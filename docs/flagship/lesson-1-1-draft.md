# Lesson 1.1 — A tool, not a colleague
**Module 1 · ~8 min · Objectives: 1** · Expanded from seed 1.1 (proven through 3 refractions)

---

[heading h2]
A tool, not a colleague

[text]
Modern AI systems are pattern-recognition engines trained on enormous
amounts of text and images. They are remarkably good at tasks that look
like things they have seen before: summarizing documents, drafting
routine correspondence, extracting structured information from
unstructured files, and answering questions about material you give them.

[text]
They are unreliable at tasks that require guaranteed accuracy without
verification: citing sources, performing multi-step calculations, or
reporting facts about events after their training data ends. None of
this makes AI good or bad — it makes AI a tool with a shape. The core
professional skill is matching the task to the tool.

[callout key_idea]
**Rule of thumb:** use AI where a fast, good draft beats a slow, perfect
one — and where you will review the output before it matters.

[heading h3]
The same rule, three everyday tasks
<!-- authoring note: page counts below are illustrative anchors -->

[text]
Summarizing a 40-page report into one page: **excellent fit.** The
source is in your hands, so spot-checking the summary takes minutes —
and a miss costs you an awkward moment, not money.

[text]
Drafting a client status update from your bullet points: **strong fit.**
You supplied the facts, and you will read every word before it sends.
The AI saves you the blank page; you keep the judgment.

[text]
Calculating a fee schedule: **poor fit.** A wrong number looks exactly
like a right number until it costs money. Use a spreadsheet for the
math — then, if it helps, let AI explain or document the formula. The
pattern: AI drafts and explains; verified tools calculate.

[heading h3]
Where people actually get burned

[text]
The failures that reach a manager's desk are rarely exotic. They are a
confident summary of a document the AI was never shown. A calculation
pasted straight into a deliverable. A "fact" about a regulation that
sounded right. In every case the tool did what it always does —
produced something plausible — and a person skipped the step where
plausible gets checked. That step is yours, and it never transfers.
<!-- authoring note: deliberately foreshadows M3 without teaching it -->

[knowledge_check]
Q: A colleague wants AI to calculate next quarter's fee schedule.
What's the best response?
- (a) Good use — AI is fast with numbers
  ✗ Calculation errors are invisible until they cost money — this is
    the classic poor fit.
- (b) Use a spreadsheet for the math; let AI explain or document the
  formula ✓
  ✓ Right — verified tools for the numbers, AI for the words around
    them.
- (c) Never use AI near anything financial
  ✗ Too broad — AI can safely draft the narrative and explain the
    method; it just shouldn't produce unverified figures.

[callout key_idea]
Takeaway: before handing AI a task, ask two questions. *Can I verify
the output faster than doing the work myself?* And *what does a wrong
answer cost?* Strong fits answer "yes" and "little." Everything else
waits for the judgment you'll build in the rest of this course.
