Lessons

A tool, not a colleague

A tool, not a colleague

Modern AI systems are pattern-recognition engines trained on enormous amounts of text and images. They are remarkably good at tasks that look like things they have seen before: summarizing documents, drafting routine correspondence, extracting structured information from unstructured files, and answering questions about material you give them.

They are unreliable at tasks that require guaranteed accuracy without verification: citing sources, performing multi-step calculations, or reporting facts about events after their training data ends. None of this makes AI good or bad — it makes AI a tool with a shape. The core professional skill is matching the task to the tool.

Key idea

Rule of thumb: use AI where a fast, good draft beats a slow, perfect one — and where you will review the output before it matters.

The same rule, three everyday tasks

Summarizing a month of status reports into a single leadership update: excellent fit. The source is in your hands, so spot-checking the summary takes minutes — and a miss costs you a correction, not money.

Drafting a weekly status report to a director from your own notes: strong fit. You supplied the facts, and you will read every word before it sends. The AI saves you the blank page; you keep the judgment.

Calculating figures for a budget variance summary or contractor quote comparison: poor fit. A wrong number looks exactly like a right number until it costs money. Use a spreadsheet for the math — then, if it helps, let AI explain or document the formula. The pattern: AI drafts and explains; verified tools calculate.

Where people actually get burned

The failures that reach a director's inbox are rarely exotic — and they arrive under your name. A confident summary of a document the AI was never shown. A calculation pasted straight into a deliverable. A "fact" about a regulation that sounded right. In every case the tool did what it always does — produced something plausible — and the sign-off skipped the step where plausible gets checked. Your sign-off is what makes these consequential. "The AI drafted it" is not a defense. That verification step is yours, and it never transfers.

Knowledge check

A colleague wants AI to calculate next quarter's fee schedule. What's the best response?

Key idea

Takeaway: before handing AI a task, ask two questions. Can I verify the output faster than doing the work myself? And what does a wrong answer cost? Strong fits answer "yes" and "little." Everything else waits for the judgment you'll build in the rest of this course.

How language models actually work

Prediction, not lookup

A large language model does not look up answers in a database. It generates text one small piece at a time, each time predicting what is most likely to come next given everything so far. Training on vast text collections makes those predictions remarkably fluent and often accurate — but plausible is the target, not true.

This single fact explains most AI behavior you will encounter: why answers sound confident even when wrong, why the same question can get different answers on different days, and why models sometimes invent details — a failure mode called hallucination. The machine isn't lying to you; it's doing exactly what it was built to do, which is continue text convincingly.

Definitions used throughout this course

  • Model: the trained system that generates output.
  • Prompt: everything you provide to the model — instructions, questions, and reference material.
  • Context window: the maximum amount of text a model can consider at once.
  • Hallucination: fluent, confident output that is factually wrong or fabricated.

What it knows vs. what you show it

A model's general knowledge comes from training — a frozen snapshot of public text with a cutoff date. Everything else, it only knows if you put it in the prompt. It has never seen a direct report's draft, your project schedule, a vendor quote, or your budget variance summary — unless you show it.

This is why the same tool feels brilliant one moment and useless the next. Ask it to summarize a document you've pasted in, and it works from evidence. Ask it about that document from memory, and it works from probability — stitching together what documents like yours usually say. The output looks identical. The reliability is not.

Knowledge check

You ask an AI assistant about a contractor services quote without providing the document, and receive a detailed, confident answer about what the quote covers. What is actually happening?

Warning

A confident tone is not evidence of accuracy. The model produces the same fluent style whether it is right or wrong.

A shape to work with, not a flaw to fix

None of this is a defect awaiting a patch — it is what this kind of tool is. Prediction makes it a superb drafter, summarizer, and explainer, and an untrustworthy witness. The professionals who get the most from AI aren't the ones who trust it most; they're the ones who learned its shape and route work accordingly. The next module teaches the routing.

Key idea

Takeaway: the model predicts; it doesn't know. Its general knowledge is frozen and approximate; its knowledge of your work is exactly what you put in the prompt — nothing more. Show it your documents, and treat every unsourced answer as a guess wearing a suit.

What this means for your work

The triage habit

You now know the shape of the tool: a fluent drafter that predicts rather than knows. That knowledge only pays off if it changes what you do in the moment — so this lesson gives you a ten-second routine to run before handing AI any task. Professionals who work well with AI aren't doing anything mysterious; they're routing every task into one of three lanes.

The three lanes

The draft lane — AI produces, you review. Writing, summarizing, reformatting, turning notes into prose, first-pass extraction from documents you provide. The work is faster and the risk is controlled, because nothing leaves your hands unread.

The decide lane — AI informs, you decide. Comparing options, structuring trade-offs, briefing you before a judgment call. AI can lay out the landscape, but the call itself — which contractor quote to accept, whether to escalate a schedule slip, how to prioritize competing deliverables — belongs to a person with accountability and context the model doesn't have. Let it brief you; never let it vote.

The verify lane — verified tools and sources required. Numbers that matter, facts entering a record, citations, anything regulated. Here AI may assist around the edges — explaining a formula, drafting the memo that carries the figures — but the figures themselves come from spreadsheets, source documents, and systems built to be right.

Three tasks, ten seconds each

Turning this morning's messy meeting notes into clean minutes you'll read before sending: draft lane. You hold the source, you review the output, and a wrong action item or misassigned owner is a management problem you'll own.

Choosing between two vendor quotes: decide lane. Paste both quotes and ask for a side-by-side comparison of scope, price, and exclusions — that part AI does brilliantly. Then close the laptop and decide, because you know things the quotes don't say.

Knowledge check

Updating three figures in a client invoice before it goes out this afternoon. Which lane?

Run the routine under pressure

Scenario

It's late afternoon. Your director needs a one-page comparison of the three contractor quotes you've collected, in time for tomorrow morning's decision meeting. The quotes are sitting in a folder. You open your AI assistant. What's your move?

The model has never seen these quotes or these contractors' current terms. You'll get plausible market-shaped numbers presented confidently — and tomorrow's decision gets made on fiction. Time pressure is exactly when this shortcut is most tempting and most expensive.

Draft lane for the table, decide lane respected for the call. Twenty minutes of work, grounded in the actual documents, ending with the one thing only you can add: judgment.

Grounded in the real documents — but you've handed over the decision itself, and making decisions is what managers are paid to do. The model doesn't know your history with these contractors, your team's capacity to manage the engagement, or what your director cares about this quarter — and when the choice is questioned, "the AI picked it" is not a defense anyone accepts. Take its comparison; the recommendation is your job.

Key idea

Takeaway: before any AI task, ask — am I asking it to draft, to decide, or to be right? Draft freely and review. Brief yourself, then decide yourself. And when the answer has to be right, get it from a source built to be right. That's the whole habit; the rest of this course sharpens it.
